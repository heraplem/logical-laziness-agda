\paragraph{Lazy cost analysis.}
There are many other semantics for lazy programming languages. The best known of
these is the natural semantics of Launchbury~\cite{launchbury1993}, which models
the state of a lazy program as a heap of variable bindings that is updated
whenever a variable is evaluated. This semantics has the advantage of being easy
to understand intuitively, as it closely reflects a na{\"{i}}ve implementation
of lazy evaluation on an actual computer.

Clairvoyance semantics was first studied by Hackett and
Hutton~\cite{clairvoyant}, who proved its equivalence with Launchbury's
semantics. Li \emph{et al.} developed a model of this semantics in Rocq, along
with a framework for automating proofs about the behavior of
programs~\cite{forking-paths} in it.

Demand semantics was first studied by Bjerner and
Holmstr{\"{o}}m~\cite{bjerner1989}. Their semantics was untyped; a typed version
was first published by Xia \emph{et al.}~\cite{demand-semantics}. Our demand
semantics is essentially the same as theirs. Xia \emph{et al.} were also the
first to demonstrate that demand semantics is equivalent to other semantics for
lazy languages; in particular, they proved the cost existence and cost
minimality theorems, which, together, amount to equivalence with clairvoyance
semantics.

\paragraph{Functional logic programming.}
We implemented our examples in Curry~\cite{curry},
however most of the techniques in this paper are not specific to Curry
and could just as easily have been implemented in other functional logic languages
such as Verse~\cite{verse} or mercury~\cite{mercury}.
Because $\LL$ uses eager evaluation, we could even use a pure logic language like
prolog for the translation~\cite{EagerFLPIsLogic}.
The only technique that is specific to
Curry is traversing the search tree~\cite{DBLP:journals/jflp/BrasselHH04}.

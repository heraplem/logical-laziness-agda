\subsection{The Base Language \(\LB\)}\label{subsec:base-lang}

We show the syntax of the base language \(\LB\) in \cref{fig:syntax-base}.
Language \(\LB\) is a simply typed calculus with booleans, lists, and lazy
thunks (\ie~suspended computations). Its terms include variables,
\(\tletZ\)-bindings, conditionals, and recursion based on \(\tfoldrZ\). In
addition, it contains \( \tlazyZ \) and \( \tforceZ \) operators, which wrap a
computation in a lazy thunk and force a computation from a thunk,
respectively.
% The \(\TThunkZ\) type represents thunks in lazy evaluation.
Finally, the \( \ttickZ \) operator incurs one unit of computation cost. These
explicit operators decouple our analysis from any particular evaluation model.

Note that language \(\LB\) does not have higher-order functions or general recursion
because demand semantics does not support these
features~\cite{bjerner1989,demand-semantics}.

  \tnil{} // \tcons{t}{t} // \tfoldr{x}{x}{t}{t}{t} // \tlazy{t} // \tforce{t} //
  \ttick{t}
\end{bnf}

Most of the typing rules for language \(\LB\) are standard, so we omit them. We show the
typing rules for thunks and computation costs in \cref{fig:typing-base}.
Notably, lists are lazy: the cons of lists \(\tcons{t_1}{t_2}\) takes a term
\(t_1\) of type \(A\) and a term \(t_2\) of type $\TThunk{\texttt{(\(\TList{A}\))}}$, so
the tail of a list must always be wrapped in a \(\TThunkZ\). Similarly, the
``function'' argument to \(\tfoldrZ\) always requires that its second parameter
be wrapped in a \(\TThunkZ\).

We can give language \(\LB\) a denotational semantics \(\EE{t}{\gamma}\) for any
term \(t\) in an environment \(\gamma\) by simply ignoring all operations
related to lazy evaluation and computation costs:
\[
  \EE{\tlazy{t}}{\gamma} = \EE{\tforce{t}}{\gamma} = \EE{\ttick{t}}{\gamma} = \EE{t}{\gamma} .
\]

\subsection{The Logic Language \(\LL\)}\label{subsec:logic-lang}

\begin{figure}

We show the syntax of language \(\LL\) in \cref{fig:syntax-logic}. Compared to
\(\LB\), language \(\LL\) has more types: unit \(\TUnit\), natural
numbers \(\TNat\), and products \(\TProd{A}{B}\). \ifextended We need the
  natural number type to model computation costs, and we need product types to
  return computation results along with costs. Since we are going to model
  demand semantics, we also need products to model evaluation contexts, and we
  need the unit type to model \emph{empty} contexts. \else We need natural
  numbers and products to represent computation costs associated with values,
  and we need products and the unit type to represent environments. Note that
  the types of language \(\LL\) are a superset of the types of \(\LB\). As a consequence,
  any valid typing context for \(\LB\) is also a valid typing context for
  language \(\LL\). \fi

Language \(\LL\) has introduction and elimination forms for all of the new
types. In particular, the \(\tlazyZ\) and \(\tforceZ\) operators from \(\LB\)
have been replaced by the \(\tthunkZ\) and \(\tundefined\) constructors and the
\(\tcaseZ\) eliminator. The \(\tthunkZ\) operator reduces its argument and wraps it in a
\(\sthunkZ\) value; \(\tundefined\) reduces to the special value
\(\sundefined\); \(\tcaseZ\) discriminates between these two cases. In the
\transCname{}, the \(\tthunkZ\) and \(\tundefined\) constructors indicate whether a computation is evaluated
or not; in the \transDname, they indicate whether a computation is demanded or
not.

Language \(\LL\) also has constructs for logic programming. Free terms
(\(\tfreeZ\)) may evaluate to any value of a specified type; they can be thought
of as ``anonymous logic variables''. The choice operator (\(\tchooseZ\))
evaluates nondeterministically to either of its operands. Failure (\(\tfail\))
never evaluates to anything.

Finally, \(\LL\) has two new relational operators, \(\teqZ\) and \(\tleZ\). The
\(\teqZ\) operator indicates equality, as usual. The \(\tleZ\) does \emph{not}
indicate the standard order on natural numbers; rather, it indicates the \emph{definedness} relation. Given two values \(a, a'\), we write \(a \le a'\), pronounced ``\(a\) is less defined than \(a'\)'', if \(a\) has the same shape as \(a'\), but with some \(\sthunkZ\) parts possibly replaced by \(\sundefined\).

\paragraph{Derived terms in \(\LL\).}
We use \(\LL\) to implement functional logic programs that ``carry'' a
computation result and its associated computation cost. To encapsulate
operations related to computation costs, we define some derived term constructs
in \(\LL\) in \cref{fig:logic-derived}. We use \treturn{t} to lift \(t\) to a
computation that costs \(0\). We use \tdo{x}{t_1}{t_2} to chain computations
\(t_1\) and \(t_2\) while accumulating their costs. We use
\tfoldM{x_1}{x_2}{t_1}{t_2}{t_3} to chain a list of computations, accumulating
all of their costs. The \tfoldMZ{} operation employs an auxiliary construct
\ttransposeZ, which converts terms of type $\TThunk{\texttt{(\(\TM{A}\))}}$ to terms of
type $\TM{\texttt{(\(\TThunk{A}\))}}$. In addition, we define \tassertZ{}, which ensures
that a given condition holds, causing the computation to fail otherwise.

We show all the typing rules related to these derived terms in
\cref{fig:logic-derived}.

\subsection{\TransCname{} and \TransDname{}}\label{subsec:clairvoyance-translation}\label{subsec:demand-translation}

We define the \transCname{} \(\transC{\cdot}\) in \cref{fig:transC}. The
\transCname{} translates terms in \(\LB\) to terms in \(\LL\) that simulate
clairvoyance semantics. Values and variables are translated using \treturnZ.
Variable bindings~(\tletZ) and conditionals~(\tifZ) are translated via
sequencing (\tdo{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}). Folds (\tfoldrZ{}) are translated to the
derived term construct \tfoldMZ. Readers familiar with monads might recognize
that this is essentially a monadic
translation~\cite{moggi-monad,malias,wadler-monad} with adaptations for the
\TLazyZ{} type~\cite{forking-paths}.

The most important cases of the translation are those for \(\tlazyZ\) and
\(\tforceZ\). When the original term is \(\tlazy{t}\), we use the
nondeterministic choice operator \texttt{?} to either skip the inner term
(\(\treturn{\tundefined}\)) or evaluate the inner term and wrap the result
inside a \tresultZ. When the original term is \(\tforce{t}\), we first evaluate
\(t\) and then pattern match on the result \(y_1\): if \(y_1\) evaluates to
\(\tresult{y_2}\), then we return \(y_2\); otherwise, we \tfail. Note that
\tfail{} only terminates the current computation branch, so any branches where
\(y_1\) evaluates to a \tresultZ{} will continue. To \ttickZ{} a computation, we
simply run it and increment its accumulated cost by \(1\).

We also define the \transDname{} \(\transD{t}{\Gamma}\) from \(\LB\) to \(\LL\)
in \cref{fig:demand-translation}. In general, the \transDname{} operates on open
terms with an arbitrary number of free variables, but we can build an intuition
for how it works by considering terms with just one free variable.

Let \(t(x)\)
denote a ``function'' of \(x\) (\ie{} a term in \(\LL\) with one free variable
\(x\)). Intuitively, the demand semantics interpretation of \(t\) should be a
``function'' \(t'(x, y)\) that takes, in addition to the original parameter
\(x\), a new \emph{demand} parameter \(y\) representing ``how much'' of \(t(x)\)
will ultimately be needed, and it should compute ``how much'' of \(x\) is needed
in order to produce \(y\). From this specification, we can see that \(t'(x, y)\)
acts like an \emph{inverse} of \(t(x)\) in the parameter \(y\) in that it uses the \emph{output} of \(t(x)\) to compute ``how much'' of the \emph{input} it used. In general,
any term \(t(x)\) has a canonical inverse term \(t^{-1}(y)\) given by
\[
  t^{-1}(y) = \tlet{x}{\tfree{A}}{\tassert{\teq{t(x)}{y}}{x}} .
\]
However, \(t'(x, y)\) cannot produce just any value inverse to \(y\):
it must produce one that is less defined than \(x\). We add this as a
constraint:
\[
  \begin{array}{rcl}
    t'(x, y) & = & \tlet{x'}{\tfree{A}}{\relax} \\
             &   & \tassert{\tle{x'}{x}}{\relax} \\
             &   & \tassert{\teq{t(x')}{y}}{\relax} \\
             &   & x' .
  \end{array}
\]
This is most of the way to the \transDname{}. The missing piece is that, being a
\emph{translation}, the \transDname{} must turn terms in \(\LB\) into terms in
\(\LL\). We can achieve this by composing \(t'\) with some previously-defined
translation. As we show in \cref{sec:equiv}, the \transCname{} is exactly
the translation that we need. After adjusting for the fact that the
\transCname{} produces terms of type \(\TMZ\), we arrive at the single-variable
case of the \transDname{}:
\[
  \begin{array}{rcl}
    \transD{t}{\relax}(x, y) & = & \tlet{x'}{\tfree{A}}{\relax} \\
                             &   & \tassert{\tle{x'}{x}}{\relax} \\
                             &   & \tlet{z}{\transC{t}(x')}{\relax} \\
                             &   & \tassert{\teq{\tfst{z}}{y}}{\relax} \\
                             &   & \tpair{x'}{\tsnd{z}} .
  \end{array}
\]

We prove that \transCname{} and \transDname{} preserve the well-typedness of the
original term in \(\LB\):
\begin{theorem}
  If \( \TB{\Gamma}{t}{A} \), then \( \TL{\Gamma}{\transC{t}}{\TM{A}} \).
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:transD-type}
  If \( \TB{\Gamma}{t}{A} \), then \( \TL{\Gamma, x : A}{\transD{t}{\Gamma}}{\TM{\verts{\Gamma}}} \).
\end{theorem}

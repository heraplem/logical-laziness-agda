\section{The Translation}\label{sec:translation}

In this section, we define two languagues: the base language \(\LB\), a simple
functional langugae~(\cref{subsec:base-lang}); and a simple functional logic
language \(L\)~(\cref{subsec:logic-lang}). Next, we develop two translations
from \(LB\) to \(LL\): a \emph{\transCname} strategy, which produces terms
modeling clairvoyance
semantics~\cite{clairvoyant,forking-paths}~(\cref{subsec:clairvoyance-translation});
and a \emph{\transDname}, itself a thin wrapper around \transCname, which
produces terms modeling demand
semantics~\cite{bjerner1989,demand-semantics}. Finally, we demonstrate the
translation by applying it to the example of \texttt{take} and \texttt{append}
discussed in \cref{sec:motivating-example}.

We prove that this translation strategy is correct with respect to the
clairvoyance semantics and the demand semantics in Agda, but we defer details of
the correctness theorem, its proof, and the Adga development to
\cref{sec:equiv}.

\subsection{The Base Language \(\LB\)}\label{subsec:base-lang}

We show the syntax of the base language \(\LB\) in
\cref{fig:syntax-base}. \(LB\) is a simple typed calculus with booleans, lists,
and lazy thunks. Its terms include variables, let-bindings, conditionals, and
recursion based on \(\tfoldrZ\). In addition, it contains the explicit
\( \tlazyZ \) and \( \tforceZ \) for wrapping a computation in a lazy thunk and
forcing a computation in a thunk, respectively. The \(\TThunkZ\) type represents
thunks in lazy evaluation. Finally, the \( \ttickZ \) operator incurs one unit
of computation cost. These explicit operators decouple our analysis from any
particular evaluation model.

\(\LB\) does not support functions or general recursion. This is because demand
semantics does not support those features~\cite{bjerner1989,demand-semantics}.

Most of the typing rules for \(\LB\) are standard, so we omit them. We show the
typing rules for thunks and computation costs in \cref{fig:typing-base}.
Notably, lists are lazy: the cons of lists \(\tcons{t_1}{t_2}\) takes a term
\(t_1\) of type \(A\) and a term \(t_2\) of type \(\TThunk{(\TList{A})}\), so
the tail of a list must always be wrapped in a \(\TThunkZ\). Similarly, the
``function'' argument to \(\tfoldrZ\) always requires its second paramter be
wrapped in a \(\TThunkZ\).

\paragraph{Evaluation semantics.}
We can give language \(\LB\) a denotational semantics \(\EE{t}{\gamma}\) for any
term \(t\) in an environment \(\gamma\) by simply ignoring all operations
relating to lazy evaluation and computation costs:
\[
  \EE{\tlazy{t}}{\gamma} = \EE{\tforce{t}}{\gamma} = \EE{\ttick{t}}{\gamma} = \EE{t}{\gamma} .
\]

We do not need to model the natural semantics of \(\LB\), because prior works
have established equivalence in terms of computation cost between natural
semantics and clairvoyance semantics~\cite{clairvoyant}, and between
clairvoyance semantics and demand semantics~\cite{demand-semantics}. We discuss
more details of those semantics in \cref{sec:equiv}.

\subsection{The Logic Language $L$}\label{subsec:logic-lang}

We show the syntax of \(\LL\) in \cref{fig:syntax-logic}. Compared with \(\LB\),
language \(\LL\) has some new types: a unit type \(\TUnit\), natural numbers
\(\TNat\), and product types \(\TProd{A}{B}\).
\ifextended
We need the natural number type to model
computation costs, and we need product types to return computation results along
with costs. Since we are going to model demand semantics, we also need products
to model evaluation contexts, and we need the unit type to model \emph{empty}
contexts.
\else
We need natural numbers and products to represent computation costs. 
\fi

In addition, we replace the \(\TThunkZ\) type with a \(\TLazyZ\) type, which can
be either \(\tundefined\) or a \(\tresult{v}\) for some \(v\). In the
\transCname{}, these two constructors indciate whether a computation is
evaluated or not; in the \transDname, they indicate whether a computation is
demanded or not.

\(\LL\) has introduction and elimination forms for all of the new types.  We
have no need for arbitrary functions on natural numbers, so we include only
literals and addition. We also have constructs for logic programming: free
variables~(\(\tfreeZ\), also known as logic variables), nondeterministic
choice~(\(\tchooseZ\)), and failure~(\(\tfail\)). Note that free variables must
be annotated with their types in order to ensure deterministic typing. Finally,
we have a Boolean operator \(\tleZ\), which indicates whether its left-hand
operand lies below its right-hand operand in the order of approximations.

\paragraph{Derived terms in \(\LL\).}
We intend to use \(\LL\) to implement functional logic programs that ``carry'' a
computation result and an associated computation cost. To simplify this, we
define some derived term constructs in \(\LL\)
(\cref{fig:logic-derived}). \treturn{t} lifts \(t\) to a computation that costs
\(0\); \tdo{x}{t_1}{t_2} chains the computations \(t_1\) and \(t_2\),
accumulating their costs; and \tfoldM{x_1}{x_2}{t_1}{t_2}{t_3} chains a list of
computations, accumulating all of their costs. \tfoldMZ employs an auxiliary
construct \ttransposeZ, which converts terms of type \(\TThunk{(\TM{A})}\) to
terms of type \(\TM{(\TThunk{A})}\). \tassertZ ensures that a given condition
holds by causing the computation to diverge if it does not.

We show all the typing rules related to these derived terms in
\cref{fig:logic-derived} as well. Readers who are familiar with monads might
recognize that \texttt{M} is a writer's monad~\cite{moggi-monad,wadler-monad}.

\subsection{The \TransCname}\label{subsec:clairvoyance-translation}

\(\transC{-}\) (\cref{fig:transC}) translates from \(\LB\) to terms in \(\LL\)
that simulate clairvoyance semantics. Values and variables are translated using
\treturn. \tletZ and \tifZ expressions are translated using
\tdo{\cdot}{\cdot}{\cdot}. \tfoldrZ, is translated to the derived term construct
\tfoldMZ. Readers familiar with monads might recognize that this is essentially
a monadic translation~\cite{moggi-monad,malias,wadler-monad}, with adaptations
for the \TLazyZ{} type borrowed from the clairvoyance
monad~\cite{forking-paths}.

The most important cases are those for \(\tlazyZ\) and \(\tforceZ\). When the
orginal term is \(\tlazy{t}\), we use the nondeterministic operator \texttt{?}
to either skip the inner term's evaluation~(\(\treturn{\tundefined}\)) or
compute the inner term and wrap the result inside \tresultZ. When the original
term is \(\tforce{t}\), we first evaluate \(t\) and pattern match on the result
\(y_1\). If \(y_1\) evaluates to \(\tresult{y_2}\), we return \(y_2\);
otherwise, we fail. (Note that \tfail{} only terminates the current
nondeterministic branch, so any branches that contain a \tresultZ{} will
continue.) To \ttickZ a computation, we simply run it and then increment its
accumulated cost by \(1\).

\begin{figure}[t]
  \begin{align*}
    \transC{x} &= \treturn{x} \\
    \transC{\tlet{x}{t_1}{t_2}} &= \tdo{x}{\transC{t_1}}{\transC{t_2}} \\
    \transC{\ttrue{}} &= \treturn{\ttrue{}} \\
    \transC{\tfalse{}} &= \treturn{\tfalse{}} \\
    \transC{\tif{t_1}{t_2}{t_3}} &= \tdo{y}{t_1}{\tif{y}{\transC{t_2}}{\transC{t_3}}} \\
    \transC{\tnil{}} &= \treturn{\tnil{}} \\
    \transC{\tcons{t_1}{t_2}} &= \tdo{y_1}{\transC{t_1}}{\tdo{y_2}{\transC{t_2}}{\treturn{(\tcons{y_1}{y_2})}}} \\
    \transC{\tfoldr{x_1}{x_2}{t_1}{t_2}{t_3}} &= \tdo{y}{\transC{t_3}}{\tfoldM{x_1}{x_2}{\transC{t_1}}{\transC{t_2}}{y}} \\
    \transC{\tlazy{t}} &= \tchoose{\treturn{\tundefined}}{\tdo{y}{t}{\treturn(\tresult{y})}} \\
    \transC{\tforce{t}} &= \tdo{y_1}{t}{\tcase{y_1}{y_2}{\treturn{y_2}}{\tfail}} \\
    \transC{\ttick{t}} &= \tlet{u}{\transC{t}}{\tpair{\tfst{u}}{\tsnd{u} + 1}}
  \end{align*}
  \caption{The \transCname{} \(\transC{-}\) from \(\LB\) to \(\LL\).}\label{fig:transC}
\end{figure}

The \transCname{} preserves well-typedness of the original term in \(\LB\):
\begin{theorem}
  If \( \TB{\Gamma}{t}{A} \), then \( \TL{\Gamma}{\transC{t}}{\TM{A}} \).
\end{theorem}

\subsection{The \TransDname}\label{subsec:demand-translation}

We now define the \transDname{} \( \transD{t}{\Gamma} \) from \( \LB \) to
\( \LL \) (\cref{fig:demand-translation}), which produces terms that simulate
the demand semantics. Unlike the \transCname, the typing context \(\Gamma\) in
\( \LB \) is also a parameter to the translation, because we need to introduce
new free variables with types corresponding to types in \( \Gamma \). This is
consistent to the demand translation of Xia \textit{et
  al.}~\cite{demand-semantics}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \[
    \begin{array}{rl}
      \transD{t}{x_1 : A_1, \ldots, x_n : A_n} = &\tlet{x'_1}{\tfree{A_1}}{\relax} \\
                                                 &\tassert{\tle{x'_1}{x_1}}{\relax} \\
                                                 &\multicolumn{1}{c}{\vdots} \\
                                                 &\tlet{x'_n}{\tfree{A_n}}{\relax} \\
                                                 &\tassert{\tle{x'_n}{x_n}}{\relax} \\
                                                 &\tlet{u}{\transC{t'}}{\relax} \\
                                                 &\tassert{\teq{x}{\tfst{u}}}{\relax} \\
                                                 &\tpair{\texttt{(\( x'_1 \),\ \( \ldots \),\ \( x'_n \))}}{\tsnd{u}} \\
      \multicolumn{2}{c}{\text{where \( t' = t[x'_1/x_1, \ldots, x'_n/x_n] \).}}
    \end{array}
  \]
  \caption{The \transDname.}\label{fig:demand-translation}
\end{figure}

The \transDname{} \( \transD{t}{\Gamma} \) walks through \(\Gamma\), declares a
free variable for every variable in \(\Gamma\), and asserts that the free
variable approximates (\texttt{<=}) the corresponding variable in \(\Gamma\). It
then substitues all the original variables in \(t\) with newly declared free
variables to obtain a new term \(t'\), and passes \(t'\) to the \transCname{}
\(\transC{-}\).

Intuitively, the \transDname{} \emph{inverses} the translated code obtained from
the \transCname{} using free variables, but additionally constrains the context
of \(\transC{t'}\) to approximate the original \(\Gamma\).\ly{TODO: I don't like
  this explanation. Find a better one later.}

The \transDname{} preserves well-typedness of the original term in \(\LB\):
\begin{theorem}
  If \( \TB{\Gamma}{t}{A} \), then \( \TL{\Gamma, x : A}{\transD{t}{\Gamma}}{\TM{\verts{\Gamma}}} \).
\end{theorem}
where the operation \( \verts{-} \) turns contexts into product types:
\[
  \verts{x_1 : A_1, \ldots, x_n : A_1} = \TProd{A_1}{\TProd{\cdots}{A_n}} ,
\]
with the degenerate case \( \verts{\varepsilon} = \TUnit \).

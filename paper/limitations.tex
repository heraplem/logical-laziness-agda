\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

As mentioned in \cref{subsec:base-lang}, neither \(\LB\) nor \(\LL\)
supports higher-order functions or general recursion. This is a limitation of
demand semantics. We leave exploration of whether research in supporting higher-order
functions and general recursions in functional logic programming can be used to
extend to demand semantics as future work.

\iffalse demand semantics does not support higher-order functions or general
  recursion. The lack of higher-order functions does not fundamentally limit
  expressivity, as any higher-order program can be
  defunctionalized~\cite{DBLP:conf/ppdp/DanvyN01,DBLP:conf/acm/Reynolds72} to an
  equivalent first-order program. The lack of general recursion \emph{is} a
  fundamental limitation on expressivity, but it is not a problem for cost
  analysis: we can simulate general recursion without any change in asymptotic
  complexity by providing a ``fuel'' parameter that decreases on every recursive
  call. Nonetheless, extending demand semantics to include higher-order
  functions is an ongoing area of research. \fi

As discussed in \cref{sec:case-study}, our technique is slow with even
modestly-sized inputs. Although unfortunate, this may be acceptable. A major
motivation behind this line of research was the desire to find a lightweight
representation of demand functions that could be used to test their asymptotic
bounds (constants included) \emph{before} trying to formally verify them. For
this purpose, it usually suffices to test quite small inputs, where our
technique performs quite well in absolute (wall-clock) terms. However, some
applications seem to perform much worse than others for reasons that we do not
yet understand. Further investigation is left as future work.
